\begin{abstract}
This report presents an end-to-end machine learning study built on an open e-commerce dataset. The project is designed to mirror a practical data science workflow, beginning with data acquisition and cleaning, continuing through exploratory analysis and feature engineering, and ending with supervised model development, comparison, and selection. In addition to predictive modeling, the analysis incorporates unsupervised learning to better understand latent structure in the data and to support downstream modeling decisions. The goal of the project is not only to obtain strong predictive performance, but also to justify modeling choices, assess tradeoffs among alternative approaches, and communicate the results in a clear and reproducible way.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

E-commerce platforms generate rich behavioral and transactional data that can support a wide range of business decisions, including customer targeting, operational planning, product strategy, and service quality monitoring. Because these systems capture interactions among customers, products, payments, logistics, and outcomes, they provide a useful setting for studying the full machine learning pipeline from raw data to actionable conclusions.

In this project, we analyze an open e-commerce dataset to investigate a predictive question of practical interest. Our workflow emphasizes three ideas. First, real-world data rarely arrive in a form that is immediately ready for modeling, so data cleaning and preprocessing are essential parts of the analysis rather than minor preliminary steps. Second, exploratory analysis and unsupervised learning can reveal structure that would be difficult to detect from raw tables alone. Third, model selection should reflect not only predictive performance but also interpretability, robustness, and fit to the project context.

To address these goals, we organize the report around the major stages of an end-to-end data science workflow. We begin by describing the data source and the preparation steps needed to transform the raw data into an analysis-ready form. We then use visualization and unsupervised learning to better understand the data and motivate feature engineering choices. Next, we train and compare multiple supervised learning models using appropriate validation procedures and evaluation metrics. Finally, we select a preferred model, interpret the results, and discuss the limitations and possible extensions of the analysis.

The dataset contains information on customers, orders, order items, products, sellers, payments, geolocation, and customer reviews. \subsection{Feed-Forward Neural Network}

A feed-forward multilayer perceptron (MLP) was trained to regress \texttt{review\_score} (1--5) directly, preserving the full ordinal signal that a binary target would collapse. Unlike tree-based models, the MLP learns smooth, nonlinear interactions across the entire 145-dimensional feature space and benefits from gradient-based optimization at scale.

\paragraph{Feature pipeline.}
The feature matrix was constructed from \texttt{clean\_df\_final.csv} (108{,}911 rows). All identifier and timestamp columns were dropped. Four categorical columns---\texttt{payment\_type}, \texttt{seller\_state}, \texttt{customer\_state}, and \texttt{product\_category\_name}---were one-hot encoded (missing product categories mapped to an \texttt{unknown} level), yielding 145 input features. A fresh \texttt{StandardScaler}, fitted exclusively on each fold's training partition, was applied to all numeric columns before each training run, preventing any information from validation or test partitions from influencing feature normalization.

\paragraph{Architecture.}
Each candidate MLP follows the block structure
\[
\mathbf{x} \;\longrightarrow\; \bigl[\,\mathrm{Linear} \to \mathrm{BatchNorm} \to \mathrm{Act} \to \mathrm{Dropout}\,\bigr]^{L} \longrightarrow\; \mathrm{Linear}(1),
\]
where $L$ is the number of hidden layers, each of uniform width $d$. Batch normalization is applied after every linear transformation to stabilize gradient flow; dropout provides regularization. The output layer produces an unbounded scalar with no final activation, consistent with a regression target.

\paragraph{Training.}
Models were optimized with AdamW and a cosine annealing learning-rate schedule. Training minimized mean squared error (MSE). Early stopping monitored validation MSE with a patience of 10 epochs, restoring the best-weight checkpoint upon termination; the maximum epoch budget was 100.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter search.}
Because the search space spans mixed continuous, log-scale, and categorical dimensions, Bayesian optimization via Optuna's Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler was used in place of random or grid search. A MedianPruner discarded unpromising trials after two warm-up folds, reducing wasted computation. Each trial was evaluated on 3-fold cross-validation; the objective was mean fold RMSE. Twenty trials were run in approximately 43 minutes on CPU. The search space is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dnn_search}.

\paragraph{Best architecture.}
Trial 16 achieved the lowest mean 3-fold CV RMSE of 1.2189. The selected configuration is 4 hidden layers of width 90, ReLU activation, dropout rate 0.32, learning rate $9.3 \times 10^{-3}$, weight decay $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$, and batch size 2048. This model was retrained from scratch on the full 80\% training set and evaluated exactly once on the held-out 20\% test set.

The $R^2$ of 0.18 indicates that logistics and product features explain roughly 18\% of review score variance. This is modest in an absolute sense but consistent with the task: customer satisfaction incorporates latent factors---product quality, price perception, individual expectations---that are absent from the feature set. The compression of predictions toward the mean is a direct consequence of severe class imbalance (57.5\% of test rows carry a 5-star rating), which biases the regression toward high-scoring outcomes. Despite this, the monotonic per-bucket ordering demonstrates genuine signal extraction: orders with longer delays, greater buyer--seller distance, and worse recent seller performance are systematically predicted lower than orders with favorable logistics profiles.
